# 2026-10-14 - Business Communication - Motivational Interviewing

Original, high-level notes on applying William R. Miller and Stephen Rollnick's Motivational Interviewing ideas to collaborative business conversations; not a substitute for the book or clinical training.

## Core idea

Motivational Interviewing (MI) is a collaborative way to explore ambivalence and strengthen a person's own motivation for change. It draws out the person's reasons rather than supplying reasons for them.

## Useful practices

- **Partnership:** Work with the other person, not on them.
- **Acceptance:** Respect their perspective and agency without pretending to agree.
- **Compassion:** Keep their interests in view; do not use listening as a concealed influence tactic.
- **Evocation:** Ask what matters to them and what they see as possible.
- **Open questions:** Invite the person to explain what is important, difficult, or uncertain.
- **Affirmations:** Recognize specific effort, values, or strengths without flattery.
- **Reflections:** Paraphrase what you heard and allow correction.
- **Summaries:** Gather the person's points before moving to options or action.

## Planning checklist

1. State the real purpose of the conversation and any fixed requirements.
2. Ask permission before offering advice or a proposal.
3. Explore what the person values, wants, and finds difficult.
4. Reflect both sides of any ambivalence without forcing a resolution.
5. Ask what change, if any, the person wants to make and what would help.
6. Summarize their view, then agree on a voluntary next step or state the remaining constraint plainly.

## Guardrails

- Do not use MI language to disguise a predetermined decision or manufacture buy-in.
- In a workplace, treat colleagues as partners, not patients; use only the collaborative listening practices that fit the situation.
- When a policy or deadline is fixed, say so clearly and invite input only on what is genuinely open.
